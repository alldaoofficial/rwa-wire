A tokenized fund is a traditional pooled investment vehicle — a money market fund, a private credit fund, occasionally a broader strategy fund — that issues one of its share classes as a blockchain-based token rather than, or in addition to, a conventional book-entry share.

## What changes, and what doesn't

The fund's investment strategy, manager obligations, fee structure and regulatory registration are unaffected by tokenization. What changes is the record-keeping and transfer mechanism for that particular share class: instead of a transfer agent updating a traditional register, ownership is recorded and moved on a blockchain, often with the transfer agent function still involved in an oversight or reconciliation role.

## Where adoption has concentrated

Money market funds have been the most visible category, largely because their stable net asset value and daily liquidity make them a natural fit for onchain cash-management use cases. Private credit funds have also tokenized share classes, aiming to broaden their distribution and, in some cases, allow tokens to be used as collateral in other onchain transactions.

## Investor considerations

Because the underlying fund structure is unchanged, the same due diligence that applies to any fund — manager track record, fee levels, redemption terms, liquidity risk — applies equally to its tokenized share class. The token wrapper does not substitute for reading the fund's actual offering documents.

## How does a tokenized fund work?

The fund or an authorized service provider establishes the share class and investor eligibility rules. Investors subscribe under the fund's documents, and the administrator or transfer agent records the corresponding interests. A blockchain token may represent those interests or serve as a record of them, depending on the legal design. Transfers can be restricted to approved wallets, and the fund must still process subscriptions, distributions and redemptions.

This is different from simply buying an unrestricted crypto token. Eligibility, investor protections, settlement arrangements and redemption rights depend on the fund's jurisdiction and documents.

## What does the investor actually own?

The answer depends on the structure: the investor may hold a registered fund share represented by a token, or an interest held through an intermediary. Token possession alone is not proof that the holder has a direct claim against the underlying portfolio. Check who maintains the authoritative register and whether transfers onchain automatically update legal ownership.

## Can tokenized funds be redeemed at any time?

Not necessarily. A blockchain can process token transfers around the clock while the fund accepts subscriptions and redemptions only during defined dealing windows. Banking hours, asset-market liquidity, compliance reviews and the fund's own rules may delay cash settlement. A secondary market is not guaranteed, and a quoted token price may differ from the fund's net asset value.

## Tokenized money market funds versus private credit funds

Money market funds generally invest in short-term, high-quality instruments and focus on liquidity management, although their risks and redemption terms still matter. Private credit funds may hold less-liquid loans with longer maturities, making valuation and redemptions more complex. Putting either fund onchain does not remove the underlying portfolio's liquidity or credit risk.

## Due-diligence checklist

Before considering a tokenized fund, verify the issuer and legal jurisdiction; what the token legally represents; who maintains the shareholder register; the custodian and portfolio assets; investor eligibility; fees and yield calculations; subscription and redemption windows; transfer restrictions; and the consequences of losing access to a wallet or platform.
