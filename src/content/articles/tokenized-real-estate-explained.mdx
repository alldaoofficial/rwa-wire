Real estate tokenization typically works by placing a property, or a portfolio of properties, into a legal entity — often a special purpose vehicle — and then issuing tokens that represent shares of that entity rather than direct title to the property itself.

## The appeal: fractional access

Traditional real estate investment often requires substantial capital and long holding periods. Tokenizing ownership shares can lower minimum investment sizes considerably, letting investors gain exposure to a property or portfolio with a much smaller commitment than buying property directly or investing through many traditional real estate funds.

## The liquidity gap

The pitch for tokenized real estate often emphasizes improved liquidity — the idea that a token can trade more easily than a physical property. In practice, secondary markets for most real estate tokens remain thin. A token can technically be transferred quickly on a blockchain, but finding a buyer at a fair price still depends on real demand, which for many tokenized real estate products is limited relative to established real estate investment trusts or funds.

## What stays the same

Tokenization doesn't change the operational reality of owning property: it still needs to be maintained, tenants still need to be managed, and legal title still flows through conventional property law. The token represents an economic interest in an entity that owns the real estate — not a shortcut around the practical work of managing that real estate.

## How does real estate tokenization work step by step?

First, an issuer identifies a property or portfolio and establishes the legal ownership structure. An entity such as a special-purpose vehicle (SPV) may hold title, while investors receive shares, debt claims or other contractual rights. The issuer defines transfer restrictions, disclosures, voting rights and how distributions will be handled. Only then are tokens issued to eligible investors and recorded alongside the legally authoritative ownership records.

The token is a representation of a legal or economic interest; it does not automatically transfer the property's land-registry title.

## Do token holders receive rental income?

Some structures distribute a portion of net rental income, but this is not universal. Property taxes, maintenance, management fees, vacancies, financing costs and reserves can reduce or eliminate distributable cash. Payment timing and the right to distributions must be specified in the offering documents. An advertised gross rental yield is not the same as an investor's net return.

## What can investors actually sell?

An investor may be able to transfer a token representing an SPV share or contractual claim, subject to securities rules and platform restrictions. That is different from selling the underlying building. A token marketplace may have few active buyers, wide spreads or no secondary trading at all. Even when onchain transfers are technically available 24/7, price discovery and legal settlement may remain constrained.

## Tokenized property versus a REIT

Both can offer exposure to real estate without direct property management by the investor. A real estate investment trust (REIT) generally owns or finances a diversified portfolio under an established legal and reporting framework. A tokenized single-property vehicle may concentrate risk in one building, tenant base or manager. Tokenization is a record-keeping and transfer technology, not a substitute for diversification or investor protections.

## Real estate tokenization risks and questions to ask

Review the property valuation and independent appraisal, legal title and encumbrances, debt seniority, management arrangements, fee waterfall, investor voting rights, cash-distribution rules, transfer restrictions, expected holding period, redemption or exit options, custody arrangements and applicable securities laws. Ask what happens if the property manager, token issuer or trading platform fails.
