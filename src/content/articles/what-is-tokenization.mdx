Tokenization is the process of representing rights to an asset — a share of a fund, a bond, a unit of real estate, a claim on cash — as a digital token that can be issued, transferred and settled on a blockchain.

The asset itself does not become "crypto." A tokenized Treasury bill is still a Treasury bill; a tokenized real estate fund still owns buildings. What changes is the infrastructure used to record who owns it and how that ownership moves. Instead of updating a spreadsheet, a custodian ledger or a clearinghouse batch process, ownership is updated directly on a shared, programmable ledger.

That shift matters for three practical reasons. First, settlement can happen in minutes rather than days, because there's no separate reconciliation step between buyer and seller records. Second, tokens can be programmed with rules — who can hold them, when they can transfer, what happens on a corporate action — reducing the manual work that surrounds traditional securities administration. Third, tokenized assets can be combined and moved between platforms more easily than assets locked inside a single institution's systems.

None of this requires an asset to be "crypto-native." Tokenization is best understood as a change in market infrastructure, not a change in what is being bought and sold.

## Why now

Regulatory clarity in several jurisdictions, falling blockchain infrastructure costs, and demonstrated institutional pilots — from asset managers issuing tokenized money market funds to banks settling repo transactions onchain — have moved tokenization from a research topic to a live infrastructure decision for large financial institutions.
