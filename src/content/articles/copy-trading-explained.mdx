Copy trading allows one account to automatically mirror some or all of another trader's orders according to a platform's rules.

It can make execution easier. It does **not** make trading passive in the sense of removing risk, nor does it guarantee that a follower receives the same result as the trader being copied.

## How copy trading works

A platform designates certain accounts as lead traders or strategy providers. Followers allocate capital and select a copying method.

When the lead trader opens, changes or closes an eligible position, the platform attempts to create a corresponding order for the follower.

The follower's position may be calculated as a fixed amount per trade or proportionally to account size, depending on the platform.

## Why follower results can differ

Copying is not identical execution.

The lead trader's order reaches the market first. The follower order follows according to the platform's system. During that interval, price and available liquidity can change.

This creates **slippage**.

Other differences can come from:

- different account balances;
- minimum order sizes;
- leverage limits;
- margin availability;
- fees;
- funding payments;
- risk settings;
- failed copy orders; and
- when the follower started copying.

A displayed lead-trader return should therefore not be interpreted as the return every follower actually received.

## ROI is not enough

A very high historical ROI is visually attractive but tells little about how the return was generated.

A trader could produce high returns by taking extreme leverage, concentrating capital in a few positions or accepting deep drawdowns.

Useful metrics include:

**Maximum drawdown** — how far the strategy fell from a previous peak.

**Trading duration** — whether the record covers a meaningful range of market conditions.

**Number of trades** — whether the result depends on only a handful of outcomes.

**Profit and loss distribution** — whether many small wins are offset by occasional very large losses.

**Exposure and leverage** — how aggressively capital is being used.

**Assets under management / follower capital** — useful context, but not evidence that a strategy is safe.

## Win rate can be misleading

A 90% win rate sounds superior to a 55% win rate, but the size of wins and losses matters.

A strategy can win nine trades for $10 each and then lose $200 on the tenth. Its win rate is 90%, yet the series is unprofitable.

Conversely, a strategy with a lower win rate can be profitable if average gains meaningfully exceed average losses.

Win rate should therefore be considered alongside payoff size and drawdown.

## Leverage still applies

If the copied strategy trades leveraged futures, the follower is exposed to derivatives risk.

Copy trading does not transform a leveraged futures position into a lower-risk investment product. Liquidation, funding, volatility and margin rules still matter.

Read [Crypto Leverage Explained](/learn/crypto-leverage-explained/) and [Crypto Futures Explained](/learn/crypto-futures-explained/) before evaluating a leveraged copy strategy.

## Risk controls

Platforms may offer controls such as:

- maximum capital allocation;
- fixed order size;
- proportional sizing;
- account-level stop loss;
- position-level stop loss or take profit;
- maximum leverage; and
- limits on exposure per trading pair.

These tools can constrain risk but cannot eliminate it. Market gaps, slippage, liquidity shortages and platform failures can still affect execution.

## Profit sharing and fees

Some copy-trading systems allow the lead trader to receive a percentage of profitable follower results.

Followers can also incur the normal costs associated with the underlying trades, including maker/taker fees and, for perpetual futures, funding payments.

A strategy should therefore be evaluated on expected results **after** all relevant costs.

## What to check before copying a trader

A practical review should ask:

1. How long is the track record?
2. What was the maximum drawdown?
3. What leverage does the strategy normally use?
4. How concentrated are positions?
5. Does performance depend on one exceptional trade?
6. How does the strategy behave during falling markets?
7. Are losing positions closed or repeatedly averaged down?
8. What fees and profit sharing apply?
9. What happens if a copy order fails?
10. Can the follower set independent risk limits?

The goal is not to find a trader who never loses. Such a record should itself invite scrutiny. The goal is to understand the **risk process behind the return**.

## When copy trading can be useful

Copy trading can reduce the operational work required to execute another strategy. It can also provide a structured way to observe how a trader manages positions in real time.

But convenience should not be confused with diversification or professional asset management.

Following one aggressive trader can create highly concentrated risk. Following several traders can still create hidden concentration if all of them trade the same assets in the same direction.

## The bottom line

Copy trading automates **execution**, not responsibility.

The follower still chooses the trader, capital allocation and risk settings, and ultimately bears the losses.

Before focusing on headline ROI, study drawdown, leverage, history, position concentration, execution differences and costs. Those factors provide a much better picture of what is actually being copied.
