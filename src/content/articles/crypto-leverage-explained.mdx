Leverage lets a trader control a position larger than the capital posted as margin.

That sounds like a tool for increasing returns. Mechanically, however, leverage does something more neutral: **it increases exposure**. Whether that larger exposure produces a larger gain or a larger loss depends on the market.

## A simple leverage example

Suppose a trader has $1,000 available.

With no leverage, $1,000 of capital can create roughly $1,000 of market exposure.

With a $5,000 leveraged position backed by $1,000 of margin, a 1% move in the underlying represents roughly $50 of P&L before fees and funding. Relative to the $1,000 margin, that is a 5% move in account capital.

The same amplification works in both directions.

## Margin is not position size

This distinction is fundamental.

**Margin** is the collateral supporting a leveraged trade.

**Notional value** is the total market exposure of the position.

A trader who deposits $500 of margin and opens a $5,000 position is economically exposed to the $5,000 position, not merely the $500 deposit.

Trading fees and P&L are commonly tied to notional exposure.

## Why leverage can be misleading

Interfaces often present leverage as a multiplier: 2x, 5x, 10x and so on.

But two traders selecting the same leverage can take completely different amounts of risk.

One might use 10x leverage to open a small hedge representing only a modest fraction of account equity. Another might use 3x while committing nearly the entire account to one directional position.

The second trader may have more capital at risk despite selecting a lower leverage number.

A better measure is **total position exposure relative to total account equity**.

## What happens when the market moves against you?

Losses reduce the equity supporting the position.

Futures venues require a minimum amount of margin to keep a leveraged position open. If account equity falls below the relevant maintenance requirement, the position can be liquidated.

Higher leverage typically means less margin relative to the position and therefore less room for an adverse move.

Exact liquidation mechanics vary by platform and contract.

## Liquidation is not a stop loss

A stop-loss is an instruction intended to exit a position when a chosen level is reached.

Liquidation is a platform risk-control process triggered because collateral is no longer sufficient under the venue's rules.

Relying on liquidation as the planned exit means allowing the position to consume a very large share of its available margin. That is fundamentally different from defining risk before entering the trade.

## Isolated and cross margin

With **isolated margin**, a specified amount of margin supports a particular position.

With **cross margin**, available equity across the relevant account can support positions more broadly.

Cross margin can provide additional buffer, but that buffer is still the trader's capital. A losing position can therefore consume funds that were not initially thought of as part of that individual trade.

## Leverage and fees

If fees are charged on position notional, creating a larger leveraged position also increases the dollar cost of entering and exiting.

For example, doubling notional exposure generally doubles the fee base, assuming the same fee rate.

This becomes especially relevant for high-turnover strategies.

Funding payments on perpetual futures add another potential cost or credit depending on the funding rate and position direction.

## Leverage and volatility

Crypto markets can move rapidly, including outside traditional market hours.

A leverage level that appears manageable during quiet conditions can become dangerous when volatility expands. Slippage can also cause an exit to execute away from the expected price.

Risk planning should therefore consider more than a normal day's price movement.

## Position sizing before leverage

A more disciplined sequence is:

**1. Decide how much account capital can be lost if the idea is wrong.**

**2. Identify the price level that invalidates the trade.**

**3. Calculate an appropriate position size from that distance.**

**4. Use leverage only as a mechanism for obtaining the required exposure efficiently.**

This reverses the common beginner approach of selecting maximum leverage first and then deciding how large a trade to place.

## When leverage has legitimate uses

Leverage is not inherently irrational. Professional markets use leverage for hedging, basis trades, relative-value strategies and capital efficiency.

A trader holding spot exposure might use a smaller short futures position to hedge downside risk. A market-neutral strategy may hold offsetting positions. In those cases leverage can be part of risk management rather than simply a bet on larger returns.

The important distinction is whether exposure is intentional and measured.

## Questions to answer before using leverage

Before opening a leveraged trade, know:

- total account equity;
- position notional;
- margin committed;
- maximum planned loss;
- liquidation price;
- stop or invalidation level;
- maker/taker fees;
- current funding rate;
- margin mode; and
- what happens during extreme volatility.

If those numbers are unclear, the position is difficult to risk-manage.

## The bottom line

Leverage is best understood as an **exposure tool**, not a profit multiplier.

It allows more market exposure per unit of posted margin. That can make capital use more efficient, but it also means smaller market moves can create larger changes in account equity.

The useful question is therefore not “How much leverage can I use?”

It is: **“How much exposure can this account responsibly carry if the trade is wrong?”**
